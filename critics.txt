## Critical weaknesses

### 1. The assignment requires literature — you have none

This is the most important gap. The rubric explicitly says: *"find from references in the literature what these nodes represent and try to explain why they are central."* The Ythan food web is one of the most-studied food webs in ecology. Huxham et al. (1996) and Elton & Southwood have node-species mappings. Without linking node IDs to actual species names (e.g. is node 132 the harbour seal? the herring gull? the grey mullet?), your entire node interpretation is structural speculation. A marker would notice this immediately.

**Fix**: The original Ythan dataset (from Huxham, Begg & Raffaelli, 1996, *Oikos*) maps numeric IDs to species. Cross-referencing your top nodes with that paper transforms "node 132 has out-degree 0" into "the harbour seal has no predators in this web, consistent with its apex status." That's the difference between adequate and excellent.

### 2. Your "extra tool" is not original enough

The assignment says *"originality will be rewarded."* Running Louvain and greedy modularity is standard NetworkX housekeeping. The k-means on embeddings is more interesting, but you largely bury it. What you're missing is an ecological question motivating the tool choice. Food-web-specific analyses would be much stronger:

- **Cascade model / interval hypothesis**: Is the Ythan web "interval"? You could build a competition graph and test whether the overlap structure matches the cascade model prediction.
- **Robustness analysis**: Who, if removed, causes the most secondary extinctions? A simple cascade simulation is well within scope and highly relevant to conservation.
- **Motif census / triad significance profile** (which the assignment mentions explicitly): Counting directed 3-node subgraphs and comparing to a null model is a named assignment task you haven't done.

### 3. The 50 basal nodes claim deserves scrutiny

You report 50 basal nodes (in-degree = 0) in a 134-node web. That's 37% of the web with no prey — unusually high and worth investigating rather than just noting. In the Huxham version of the Ythan web this often reflects the inclusion of parasites with no recorded prey in this dataset, and also detritus/sediment categories. A single sentence noting *why* this might be so would show ecological understanding.

### 4. Betweenness is underinterpreted

You identify node 118 as both a generalist predator and a betweenness hub, but you stop there. The interesting question is: if node 118 (presumably a species like sanderling or some invertebrate feeder) were removed, which parts of the web would become disconnected? Betweenness is only meaningful if you say something about what the "bridging" implies for ecosystem stability. You note it's a "connector" but don't follow through.

### 5. The Girvan-Newman result is a red flag you ignore

Girvan-Newman gives Q ≈ 0.0 with one community of size 133. This is essentially telling you the algorithm found a nearly degenerate first split — one giant component and one isolate. You report it without comment. A marker will read that table and wonder if something went wrong. You should either note it explicitly ("the first GN split is uninformative for this graph's density; the hierarchy is meaningful only at finer resolutions") or drop it from the summary.

### 6. Closeness centrality on a directed graph needs a caveat

`nx.closeness_centrality` on a directed graph with low reachability (many pairs have no directed path) computes closeness only over reachable pairs by default, which inflates scores for nodes in small reachable subsets. With 134 nodes and 593 edges in a food web, many nodes will have limited directed reachability. You should either flag this limitation or verify that your WCC is also strongly connected (it won't be — food webs are typically not strongly connected). This is a genuine methodological issue, not just a detail.
